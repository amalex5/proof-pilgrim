the natural history of abstract objects

deep springs dispatch #3

2026-04-03

Deep Springs, California
Friday, April 3rd, 2026

It’s spring, and my pile of anecdotes and images from Deep Springs is growing faster than I can trim it into topiary. I was hoping to write more-frequent letters of more-better quality, but we can’t let the perfect be the enemy of the good. Deadlines make good writing.

This afternoon I’ve been lying in my hammock reading W.V.O. Quine for my logic class. I spent the morning on a seven-mile run through the sagebrush. I found a twisted rusty old horseshoe and ran back with it. Good training for running with poles; perfect memorabile of my time here. From my hammock I can hear abstract algebra office hours happening on my colleague Rachel’s porch.

This whole semester has been a dream come true. I can’t believe I only have another month left. I’m so, so sad to leave. I feel so, so lucky to have gotten to be here.

It’s been a frantic few weeks of visitors. Francis Fukuyama, Graeme Wood, Amia Srinivasan, and—uh—Gavin Newsom. Everywhere on campus there are copies lying around of The End of History (Fukuyama’s book) and The Right to Sex (Amia’s). I wasn’t really familiar with Amia, but everyone seems very into her; she’s an up-and-coming feminist philosopher who writes a popular and diverse column for the London Review of Books. Graeme Wood, who’s also a DS alumnus, is a foreign correspondent for the Atlantic. Except for Gavin, they were all here for a week each, giving talks and going on walks (walks are the one-on-one meeting format at DS) and joining us for the three meals a day we eat together. The governor was a different story: he showed up, basically uninvited, with a day’s notice, for a two-hour tour. (“Get away from me!,” he told me to my face.)

Jürgen Habermas died. Everyone was surprised he was still alive. No particular Deep Springs connection, but Jeff, the dean, described seeing him speak at the University of Virginia when he was a grad student in the 90s. He drank so much at the dinner afterwards Jeff thought that was going to be his end. Hannah subject-lined an email to the student body, “ŽIŽEK DIED!!!!,” not because the obnoxious Marxist philosopher had finally kicked it, but because she was annoyed at the mess in the common room and knew that this was the way to get everyone to read her email about it. “This tastes like postmodernism!,” someone described dinner last week. Deep Springs has felt like the humanities version of Nueva the entire time I’ve been here; the more nuanced take is that math is to Nueva as philosophy is to Deep Springs. It’s the subject that the kids are the most motivated, passionate, and self-confident about. Like math at Nueva, this is for reasons that are both good and bad, and has effects that are both good and bad.

Winter is long gone. Spring arrived, very fast and far too soon. The first full week back from break everything on the ranch resaturated. The dusky colors of the desert deepened: the sagebrush turned dark teal, the grass on the main circle went from gold to green. On Thursday morning the cottonwoods outside my window had fresh buds. On Friday morning they had leaves. A beautiful flower started appearing everywhere: delicate leaves in intense but almost translucent shades of white, pink, and yellow. It grows out of what I had thought, all winter long, were just generic leathery rubbery succulent ground vines. Nope: it’s the “California evening primrose.” It’s so beautiful.

Tumbleweeds make amazing lamps. One of the faculty houses has one, lacquered hanging upside down from the ceiling, a bare bulb in the center. The pattern of light and shadows through the branching structure is so cool it makes me shiver.

The worst winter across the West in decades. Not the Eastern Sierra backcountry ski season of my dreams. In Bishop temperatures hit the high 80s in mid-March. I put away my skis and went climbing with some locals. “This feels like mid-June,” they said. I haven’t seen any rattlers but everyone else keeps seeing them around campus. Spring means flowers, and spring means snakes. There are half a dozen dogs on campus, and people talk about taking them in to town for “rattlesnake training.” A dude in Bishop has a bunch of rattlesnakes and shock collars. He puts the shock collar on your dog; your dog starts sniffing at one of his rattlers; he shocks the dog; the dog learns to avoid the danger noodles.

There’s too much milk. Only half a dozen dairy cows, but they’re lactating out 120 pounds a day. Three dairy calves this year. The kids named them Augustine (there’s a Christian theology class running), D3 (there’s an abstract algebra class running)(it’s the symmetry group of an equilateral triangle), and Amia (after Srinivasan). Augustine got pneumonia and died last week. Even more milk for us. The dairy boys are trying to keep up by making as much cheese as they can, making a giant vat of hot chocolate every morning for breakfast (they put it in the walk-in after lunch and call it “chocolate milk”), and making two or three gallons of ice cream a day. Tonight: honey-pecan!

I’ve been using the sauna once a week. I hike up after dinner, light the fire, and hike back an hour later when it’s hot. Two hours of lying in the dark and the heat, candles flickering, the creak of the copper weathervane outside, the scurry of mice through the walls. On the floor is the Deep Springs brand. The floor is mostly fieldstones mortared together, but in the center are chunks of quartz forming an unmistakable Crazy T (an upside-down T, or rather, two L’s back to back, like L.L. Nunn, the DS founder). I discovered the blacksmith shop for the first time a couple weeks ago: a tin shack, right out of a western, behind the auto shop, with a blast furnace in the corner built from an old oil tank welded together and cut out. Anvils and branding irons on the floor.

We had a welcome party for Amia Srinivasan in the dorm. Luca, one of the students, asked, “Do you want to see the dead animals in the freezer?” A normal-looking kitchen fridge with a freezer on top. A pint of Ben & Jerry’s. A box of ice pops. A tray of ice cubes and a few ice packs. And some ziploc bags. One by one Luca pulled them out to show to me: a mouse, a snake (… a baby rattler missing its head), a “big bird” (it’s a raven, Luca), another bird (a woodpecker?), and another mouse. “There used to be a rabbit in here,” he said, poking around. Supposedly those are all for Harper to taxidermize. Eventually.

Gavin Newsom, in person, is exactly what you’d expect. The bomber jacket, the tight jeans, the swagger, the poses, the teeth, and yes, the hair. Jacob, who’s teaching the novel-writing workshop, was fascinated by his charisma (as a character study). Don, one of the other visiting professors, quipped: “He left no hand unshaken.”

That was my experience. I wandered up as his tour was finishing, as he was standing with half a dozen students and staffers on the main circle. Katie, the office manager/assistant dean, was running out from the main building with a stack of t-shirts and hats to give out. I wasn’t even trying to connect with him, but he turned to me and stuck out his hand. “I’m Gavin,” he said. I introduced myself—that’s when he made the joke about how I should stay away, because “math was my worst subject”—and he asked how long I’ve taught at Deep Springs. I described my semester, and how Deep Springs totally lives up to its reputation, and how the students are wonderful and the colleagues are wonderful, and how it’s just a great all-around place to work. “Remember me when I need a job!,” he said. I thought, “I’m the one who needs a job!” One semester is my term limit. I won’t be around to hire him when his own term limits kick him out of Sacramento at the end of the year. Only later did I realize the plausibly-deniable double-reference to the job he’s almost certainly going to apply for in 2028.

“What’s your favorite class?,” he asked the students. “His!,” Tahm said, pointing at me. “Is he a hard teacher?” “No, he’s amazing!,” Victoria said. “I hope I’m a hard teacher!,” I laughed. “You want hard teachers!,” the governor said.

After “Gavin” left, Keevan, one of his tour guides, spent dinner silent and shell-shocked. She explained that she was surrounded by too many people like that growing up in DC. That’s exactly what she was trying to escape coming to DS. Visiting faculty member Don just happened to catch her failing to hide her trauma at the end of the tour:

PIC TKTKKTKT

(You can just barely see me behind Keevan in that shot.)

“No one liked him before he came,” Don said, at the party for Amia that night, “and no one liked him after he came.”

When he left, a huge flock of ravens was circling the main quad. 20, at least. I’ve never seen this before. There are a few ravens who hang out a mile away at the dump, but they rarely come to the main campus—and certainly not this many of them.

There’s music constantly on campus, but none of it is prerecorded. The main room has a grand piano everyone plays. We pay some musician/geology Ph.D./climber dirtbag to come up once a week from Bishop and give lessons. One of the kids loves fiddle. Kel, in a previous life, spent three years as a CS major at Northeastern; now she’s a cowboy who plays fiddle on the steps of the main building at sunset.

“Cowboy” isn’t an exaggeration. Two or three students get chosen each year to be the cowboys. They’re the student leads of the cattle operation. Culturally it’s like being the valedictorian, or class president. After graduation spend the summer (and sometimes the fall and spring) still working the cows. They drive the cattle up into the White Mountains, spending two months living in a shack at 10,000 feet monitoring the herd. For their work they get paid $2800 a month. (Which… is more than the visiting professors make.)

There are no phones on campus. Why would there be? There’s no service. A few weeks after I got here, someone was visiting, and while eating lunch in the boarding house, they had their phone lying out on the table. I was shocked—first by seeing the phone, and second by my shock itself. A phone lying out on a table—such an ordinary occurrence! Not here. It was the first time I had seen a phone in weeks. No one talks about the lack of phones; it’s not ostentatious or self-righteous (”Oh, I don’t have a TV”); it’s just… why would you have a phone? The kids have laptops, but are always changing the severity of their self-imposed internet restrictions. When I arrived, there was no internet in the dorms, or on phones anywhere; a few weeks ago, they voted to ban it from their laptops everywhere on campus, too. (There are half a dozen public iMac terminals in the library with internet that they can use.) The boardinghouse has a phone—a landline—and the kids have long conversations on it—straight back to the 1990s.

Deep Springs was all-male for a century. After a lengthy legal battle, that changed in 2018. But it’s less that it’s coed now and more that it’s androgynous. Two or three of the 26 kids have strong femme energy; two or three have strong masc energy, but most are murkily in between. They all have the same terrible DIY shag haircut and names like “Keevan” and “Kel” authored for ambiguity. They all wear each other’s clothes. There’s a phrase, the “Bone Pile,” which refers, in increasing order of frequency, to three things: it’s a) a synonym for the dead animal dump, b) the name of the campus literary magazine, and c) the name of the giant pile of communal clothing in the dorm. “I didn’t bother bringing very many clothes here,” one of the kids said, “because everyone said I could just get clothes from the bone pile.” There are t-shirts emblazed with SUCH-AND-SUCH HIGH SCHOOL CLASS OF 1990. Provenance is a prime topic of conversation: almost every day when people walk into my class, someone says, “You’re wearing so-and-so’s shirt!” “Really? I just found this in the bone pile!” Does anything in the bone pile ever get washed? Unclear.

“Graeme still had shirts from the bone pile until a few years ago,” Graeme Wood’s wife said at dinner when he was here. (Graeme graduated in 1999.)

Even the roommate assignments cross gender lines. The bedrooms in the dorm seem to hold between two and four people each; as far as I can tell, the students’ main priority in assigning themselves to rooms is to mix first- and second-year students.

Ruby, the student with the most femme energy—who, in literally the most small-world moment of my entire life lives literally FOUR DOORS DOWN from my house in Ithaca—and she worked on the IHS student paper and knew me by reputation before I arrived here—“Oh, the cartoon? That was you?!?” LINK TKTKTKT—has gotten obsessed with plumbing. That’s been her labor assignment recently: fixing the toilets around campus. She’s decided that she shouldn’t even bother trying to get a Ph.D. in philosophy; she should just become a plumber and read Thomas Mann in her spare time. (But for the next two years, at least, she has a full merit scholarship to Williams.)

My classes are amazing. More next time. We went on a visiting faculty camping trip to the sand dunes on Friday. I’m taking advantage of the fully-equipped auto shop to do a bunch of miscellaneous maintenance tasks on my car. Amia is giving three public talks over the next week. One will be about campus free speech. I went on a cattle drive. I’ve only thrown up once from food poisoning. (The fact that norovirus doesn’t regularly invade the entire campus is a miracle.) Tonight we’re heading up to the observatory after dark to look at Jupiter and Betelgeuse and the Orion nebula. Tomorrow we’re taking the kids hiking to an abandoned mine (exploring abandoned mines is a popular campus pasttime).

Right now I’m back in my house. I’m looking out the window at students moving a wheel line across one of the alfalfa fields. Horses running through one of the fields further back. Snow quickly melting from the Palisades even further away. I’m unemployed and homeless after May 1st: “remember me when I need a job,” as Gavin said!
