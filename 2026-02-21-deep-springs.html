snow and slaughter at deep springs

2026-02-21

Deep Springs, California
Saturday, February 21st, 2026

Silence descended on the Deep Springs Valley this week in the form of powder, six inches, muffling the moos and the diesels until it was broken by a rifle shot Saturday morning at the start of slaughter.

M. came last Friday, planning to stay until Monday. But the passes over the Sierra all closed, and it wasn’t until Friday that she was able to get out. Thursday the kids asked me to reschedule our logic class (reschedule, not cancel) so that they could rebuild the eight-foot snowman they had built in the middle of the main circle Sunday night. I declined. They had a different idea: a few minutes after class ended, I saw them tying an old climbing rope to the trailer hitch of a 90s 4Runner. A plastic sled was lying nearby. They were wearing a mix of old climbing helmets, bike helmets, and a construction helmet. Safety first. I wanted to join their sledjoring, but the lunch bell had just rung, and I was hungry.

Wednesday morning the kids did a cattle drive down to the lake. The horsemanship class just ended, so this was the first cattle drive the first-years were allowed to go on. M. and I did a Subaru drive to Mammoth. Or tried to. A tractor-trailer had jackknifed just below the pass out of the Valley, blocking the road, and preventing our powder day. So we drove back to the college, I pulled out my backcountry skis, and did a short tour through the boulders behind campus, gliding down just before lunch and clunking through the boardinghouse in my ski boots. College president Andy was supposed to fly to San Francisco to meet with donors. But the other pass out of the valley was blocked by a second jackknifed tractor-trailer. John, the facilities manager and all-purpose fixer, made derisive comments about long-haul truck drivers and called in a favor with a buddy in Bishop. The buddy drove up to one side of the wrecked truck, John drove Andy down to the other side, Andy postholed around the truck with his suitcase, and the buddy drove him the rest of the way to the airport.

Sunday night, as the storm was starting, we fired up the sauna. After the day’s rains—rain, sleet, snow, intermixed; “Ithacating,” as the many Deep Spring’ers with Cornell connections call it—everything smelled like creosote on the hike. The sauna is a few hundred feet above campus, perched on the edge of a hill overlooking the valley. A Finnish visiting professor built it in the 90s. It’s wood-fired, so you have to light the fire and wait a couple hours before it’s hot enough (periodically hiking back up to re-stoke it). The building is recessed a few feet into the ground. On the front door is a cow skull with demonic horns. Inside is a mortar-and-fieldstone floor, a bucket and ladle, and a two-level bench. Behind the bench is a panoramic window, 2.35:1, framing a cinematic view of the valley. When we were there it was dark, and the only light came from the glow of the stove and the half-dozen candles burning around the room. After an hour we got up to do a cold plunge—the upper reservoir, used for both alfalfa irrigation and summer swimming, is 30 feet away—and to our surprise everything was covered in two inches of powder. Big fluffy flakes still coming down as we jumped in.

I love winter. I’ve missed this so much. I need winter.

Parts of the Sierra Nevada got ten feet of snow over the course of the week. The first real snowfall since New Year’s. Tuesday night I kept refreshing my internet feeds for more information about the avalanche at Castle Peak outside Truckee. I’ve backcountry skiied there half a dozen times. Horrible. Just horrible.

6:30 AM yoga in the boardinghouse on Wednesday. 5:30 PM Shabbat dinner at Aaron’s house on Friday. The Manischewitz is a religious exemption to what’s otherwise a dry campus. (“Give me an extra-generous pour,” said one of the kids, who had just finished her Jewish mysticism paper.) A bunch of the kids are reading The Magic Mountain for fun and want to finish together over break. Jacob has been building fires in the boardinghouse every morning, sitting in front of the fireplace and reading his students’ vignettes for his novel-writing workshop. The kids walk around wearing dirty Carhartts and carrying thick volumes of Aquinas and Spinoza; I can hear the quantum mechanics class in the classroom next to my office and sometimes I see the leftovers from the abstract algebra class in the other classroom. Everyone holds classes in their living rooms except the math/science faculty. We need the blackboards.

One of you pointed out to me the oddity of a school that’s simultaneously so (a) experimental, and (b) academically intense. Usually those anticorrelate. I think of the hippie progressive schools—Ithaca’s “alternative” high school; the crunchy colleges founded in the 60s—and I’ve never had any interest in those places, either as a student or a teacher, because they take academics so lackadaisically. Deep Springs manages to be both tiny and rigorous. I think that’s partly from a century of institutional momentum, and partly from being so small that it can be exceedingly selective. There were 500 applications this year for 13 student spots; there were 200 applicants last year for the handful of visiting professor jobs.

I’m so glad to be “out west.” It’s not that when I was younger I thought I’d never get to live in these places and do these kind of things; it’s that I didn’t even know these places and these adventures existed, in reality, outside of photographs and family stories. If I’m still in California in December I’ll celebrate a decade, cumulatively, of living here (simultaneous with turning 40).

Tonight, for Old Movie Night, Aaron is showing A Fistful of Dollars. Two weeks ago it was Yojimbo (the Kurosawa film, of which A Fistful of Dollars was Sergio Leone’s unauthorized Clint Eastwood-starring remake). Last week, for Valentine’s Day, Aaron showed The Umbrellas of Cherbourg, a 60s French movie I’d never heard of. I was bored at first (“this is just some silly French love story”), but after the first hour of setup it became a heartbreaker I can’t get out of my head.

Next week is the last week of the term. (The Deep Springs academic cadence is six “terms” per year, with the fall and spring semesters spanning two.) The end-of-term celebratory dinner will be “Brazilian Steakhouse”-themed according to an email we just got. First week of break I’m staying on campus and taking a WFR (wilderness first responder) course with some other students and faculty. Second week I’ll mostly be here, too. The time is just going by so quickly and being here feels so special. The “dairy boys” (actually women, but they still call themselves that) said they’d let Jacob and me milk the cows. I remain so impressed by all the colleagues and the kids here. Still no idea what I’m doing after May 1st. (The spirits told me “only good choices” last summer; I’m trying to keep listening to them.)

Also over spring break: I get to help butcher the cow!!! Its quarters have to hang in the walk-in for a week before we continue processing it. This morning, a dozen of us showed up by the gantry at 8 AM to help with the slaughter. A single cow, but 1300 pounds worth, and we weren’t finished until 2 PM. Gradually we turned it from a living being to abstract pieces of meat. I helped hacksaw down the spine to halve it. It was so cold when we started that the blood turned to slush when it hit the concrete pad. Skinning took two full hours. When we draped the hide over the fence to dry, it was still twitching. No joke.

That’s this week’s dispatch. I have a brilliant single-problem test for the logic kids—the problem came to mind a few days ago—that I’m going to give them on Thursday to tie together our term’s work. Learning all this baby digital electronics in that class the last few weeks has been such a delight and deserves its own letter. Bedtime for now.

love,
Andrew
