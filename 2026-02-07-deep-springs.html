four weeks at deep springs

2026-02-07

174 miles west-northwest of Las Vegas
February 7th, 2026

Dear friends, family, etc.:

I spent too many years as a student journalist to bury the lede, so: I quit my job, fled to the mountains, and am spending the semester as a visiting professor of math and philosophy at Deep Springs College. Teaching here has been a fantasy of mine for almost a decade; high expectations are hard to meet, but it’s been everything I’ve dreamed of and more.

In my calculus class half the kids arrive directly from the theology class; they’re always still engaged in intense discussions of Augustine or Aquinas or (just as likely) meta-issues about different ways they wish the seminar had gone. In my logic class, the best moment has been watching one of the kids scratching out a proof at the blackboard, wearing dirty unwashed flannel/Carhartts/cowboy boots (that’s the uniform), a giant knife sticking out of her back pocket. Deep Springs: the liberal arts college that’s also a working cattle ranch, with wannabe cowboys discussing the Great Books and wannabe philosophers breaking horses.

(I’m including on this letter a few of you former colleagues who I thought might be interested; the logorrhea is a representative example of the Alexander family epistolary tradition; not my best writing but can’t let the perfect be the enemy of the put-to-bed; feel free to share.)

Deep Springs is an hour from Bishop, but half the people I tell this to don’t even know where Bishop is. The Nevada border is a dozen miles east. Death Valley is a couple mountain ranges south. The Sierra Nevada are nearby but Deep Springs Valley, geologically, is the first of Nevada’s basin-and-range oscillations. You can get here from California or Nevada; either way, you cross a 7,000 foot pass. “SNOW NOT PLOWED 4 PM to 8 AM,” warns a sign. From the California side the road narrows to a single lane blasted through 100 feet of rock, curving back and forth, hiding oncoming traffic. The valley is 2,000 feet lower, a flat-bottomed bathtub dotted with sagebrush and tumbleweed, slanted southwest, two miles wide and a dozen miles long. Evaporation is the only way water leaves. Half the year there’s a shallow lake at one end; the rest of the year it’s a dusty playa. A two-lane state highway bisects the valley the long way. From the main road the driveway into the ranch is three-quarters of a mile long, laser-straight, and sometimes used as a landing strip.

The sunsets are beautiful, but not from the colors—these are some of the least colorful sunsets I’ve seen—they’re beautiful from the miles-long sightlines and the long rays of light and shadow from the low-angle winter sun. The ranch is ringed with cottonwoods—just spindly brown fractals this time of year. Everything is desaturated: yellows and browns and barely-greens.

From my living room you can see, peeking over the valley rim, just barely, the Palisades—a rugged subrange of the Sierra, with 14’ers and the southernmost glaciers in North America.

Deep Springs is a two-year college. The kids all transfer afterwards to the usual fancy four-year schools. Some of them have transferred from fancy schools—my logic class has a kid who spent a year at Cornell before coming to Deep Springs, and another kid who spent two years at Pomona. The Deep Springs “program,” as it were, comprises three pillars: academics, labor, and service. The kids spend the morning in classes (intense seminars about dense texts, etc.), the afternoons running the ranch (fixing fences, cooking meals, mucking stables), and the evenings in interminable committee meetings.

The nearest human settlement is Big Pine, 45 minutes away over the pass, with a few hundred people and a fire department. Bishop, the place that’s referred to when people talk about “going into town,” is a solid hour, packed with thousands of people and a supermarket.

There are eight cows for every one person in Deep Springs Valley. Of the 41 humans, 26 are students, and 15 are “staffulty.” Nine academics (the president, a dean, the long-term and visiting faculty), plus an office manager, boardinghouse (dining hall) manager, ranch manager, ranch mechanic, farm manager, and facilities manager. The non-academic staff are educators, too: their role is not to do the actual work of running the school but to supervise and guide the students in doing the so. The kids run the ranch, wrangle the cattle, do all the cooking and the cleaning—they each have a rotating labor position. “Student-run” means not just that they have opinions about how things should be done—they actually do the work.

The ranch consists of a few acres of alfalfa and 300 head of cattle. Heads of cattle? Heads of cows? Sorry, I’m from New York. Combined they pay for a third of the annual budget. The remaining thirds come from each the endowment and fundraising; the kids pay no tuition. There’s also a subsistence farm operation that provides a big chunk of what we eat. In the summer and fall all produce comes from the farm. Even in February there’s still so much kale that every meal has kale worked in. There are enough chickens for everyone to have fresh eggs each morning. Sometimes there are pigs, but the most recent ones are in the walk-in. The kids do all the slaughtering and butchering. We’re almost out of chicken, so the next chicken slaughter is in a few weeks. The ranch is a beef cattle operation (I think???), but there are also half a dozen dairy cows. We have milk both raw and pasteurized, and every derivative dairy product you can dream (yogurt, butter, half a dozen cheeses, fresh cream for your coffee). The two “dairy boys” (actually both women, but they still call themselves that) spend an hour and a half milking the cows, twice a day, every day, 4:30 to 6 am and pm. My house is between the dorm and the dairy barn and they wake me up every morning, jangling past with giant steel thermoses.

One of the dairy cows gave birth last week. Can you imagine anything cuter than a tiny jet-black newborn calf with matted fur still wobbly on its legs??? The kids debated whether to name it Brian (after the long-term natural sciences professor, finishing his six-year term this semester) or Augustine (there’s a Christian theology class running this semester). Soon it’ll be calving season and even more cows will be giving birth. We’re all required to sign up for 24-hour shifts of being on-call. In case something goes wrong you call the resident ob/gyn—Tim the crusty 70-year-old ranch manager—who shows up, puts on a shoulder-length rubber glove, and—next paragraph, please.

There’s a fire department! A DIY one! GUYS I’M GOING TO GET TO DRIVE THE FIRE TRUCK!!!! Teaching at Deep Springs has been a decade-long dream come true, but driving a fire truck will be a LIFELONG dream come true. The actual fire department is an hour away, so, as with everything else, problems are our problem. There’s a decommissioned CalFire wildland fire engine, plus a wildland tanker truck. The two kids who run the fire department spend a month getting actual wildland fire training in the summer; they then get to train whomever wants to volunteer for the fire crew. Last weekend we got to practice digging fire lines with pulaskis! (“It’s ‘cutting’ fire lines,” firefighter cousin D. told me when I texted him. City slicker!)

I don’t even begin to know what all else is on the ranch. Land not being a limiting factor gives an append-only quality to the built environment; space isn’t at a premium, so there’s no need to ever throw anything out. There’s a century’s worth of vehicles, in the full spectrum from working to not. There are two different dumps and a “dead animal pit.” The latter is as horrifying as it sounds; you know you’re getting close because there are bones scattered in a 100-foot radius. There are two reservoirs (used for swimming and irrigation; drinking water comes from a well), a solar field, a hydroelectric setup, a fancy telescope and control building that the current natural sciences professor wrangled the money for, a ramshackle wood sauna built by a Finnish visiting professor in the 90s, and a huge shop that resembles a more antiquey version of the Nueva i-Lab. (Unlike in San Mateo, there are no fire marshals here to complain about the jankiness of the welding stations.)

But it’s not just a dude ranch—Deep Springs is a legitimate college! What intellectual beasts have we slaughtered and are serving up this semester? On the menu are introduction to logic (me!), abstract algebra, analog electronics, Franz Fanon, Adam Smith, Spinoza, “Approaches to Christian Theology,” “Multidisciplinary Perspectives on Mind,” and a novel-writing workshop. That’s not all: there are also “independent studies” in calculus (me!), poetry writing, “Wittgenstein and Peirce on logic and language,” and quantum mechanics. “Independent study” is misnomic: they’re actual classes with grades, scheduled during the normal time slots, and they show up on transcripts. It seems that the “independent study” proviso in the handbook has gradually and unconsciously been hijacked into just MORE CLASSES!!!!, and everyone, students and professors, is happy and OK with this. My logic class has six kids, my calc class four; when I sat in on the Wittgenstein class to walk them through Peano arithmetic, there were six kids. The novel-writing workshop has 14, which is the Deep Springs equivalent of an inner-city classroom with 40 kids. Jacob keeps vacillating on whether to split it into two sections. Then there’s something that sounds like an actual independent study: one kid researching the history of fashion at Deep Springs (!) by going through the school’s archives. The kids all take a horsemanship class once a week to learn how to ride and wrangle. There’s an occasional marksmanship class (”The Way of the Gun”).

Plus Aaron the long-term humanities professor leads Talmud study on Sunday afternoons, and Tim the crusty ranch manager leads Bible study on Sunday evenings (both popular).

Usually once a year someone leads a reading group on the writings of L.L. Nunn, Deep Springs’ founder. Nunn made his fortune as an entrepreneur building power plants and laying electrical lines in Colorado mining towns at the turn of the 20th century; he then redirected his time and money into several educational experiments. Telluride House at Cornell was one of the first; Deep Springs College was the final. Nunn was a “lifelong bachelor” and Deep Springs was all-male until (after a long legal battle) his original trust was modified in 2018 to allow coeds. Make of that what you will.

How are my classes going??

Calculus is amazing. It’s my best teaching; I know the material so well and can improvise so perfectly to match the class; we’re using my textbook (so they get human Andrew in class and written Andrew as reference); it’s so perfect and so natural. Everything that comes out of my mouth is brilliant; it’s like I’m a 24-year-old in Phoenix again (but better). Perfect material, perfect kids, perfect intellectual culture, perfect amount of wisdom and knowledge on my end, more than making up for slower processing speed at age 39. No notes.

Logic is more stressful and chaotic. Objectively, it’s going fine; the kids are happy and learning stuff, but I know what my best teaching feels like, and it’s impossible to have your best teaching the first time you teach something. So I’m making it up as I go, navigating via intuition and impulse rather than experience. Hang on tight! But it’s exciting. We figured out propositional logic in an exploratory way and now we’re reading through W.V.O. Quine’s introductory logic textbook from the 60s. The kids love close reading and classic texts, so of the half-dozen logic texts I was paging through, this seemed most resonant with the culture. (The current president, who’s a biologist, double majored in philosophy and coincidentally wrote his senior thesis on Wittgenstein and Quine.)

I taught the kids to count on their hands in binary (now the entire school has gotten into it), and we’re detouring next week to see how far we can get building arithmetic circuits—like, adders and stuff—after all, what are transistors but NAND gates (more or less)? We’ve spent the last month thinking that logic is about “philosophy” and “mathematics” and “searching for truth,” but nope, turns out that it’s how we make computers! How’s THAT for a plot twist??? Secret agent from San Francisco I am! Then we’ll resume reading Quine and we’ll talk about quantifiers, and that should take us until spring break. (Spring break is a full fortnight—half the school spends the first week on campus doing Wilderness First Responder training.) Then… maybe some baby Bayesian reasoning? baby modal logic? Read David Foster Wallace’s insane math/philosophy of math book??? (The kids—all genders—really like DFW.)

In calculus, we’re going to read George Berkeley’s The Analyst! The Analyst is his 1734 satirical broadside against calculus and its shaky mathematical/logical/theological foundations. In the edit of calculus I’ve put together for DS we don’t have time to talk about limits in any depth—we have fourteen weeks and 28 classes to go from the very beginnings to flash-bang finale of Taylor series, with precalc review interspersed—but I keep pointing out, “hey, aren’t we actually dividing by zero here?”, which the kids are appropriately unsettled by. So I think this will be a good way to highlight that issue in more depth. I’ve never read it; it’s only 40 pages; it’s on-mission with the classical/Great Booksyness.

Intellectually, the Deep Springs culture feels like Nueva, but the humanities version, minus the millions of volts of ambient anxiety. Everyone loves school, everyone wants to be here; it’s a bunch of weirdo nerds. The first day of class, I wandered in at 10:02, and the kids were all already there, sitting around the seminar table quietly, notebooks out, looking at me, expectant. Shit. I started mumbling incoherent opening comments, and, to my horror, they started diligently taking notes on what I was saying. I’m so used to the Nueva chaos of everyone wandering in at random times and no one taking notes and me screaming to get attention. (To be clear, I like chaos, and none of this was stuff I minded about Nueva!)

The Deep Springers are incredibly good readers and discussants. They’re vocal about their love of the St. John’s-style seminar. If anything, Deep Springs has the opposite problem from Nueva: the kids are so eager to talk about everything ad infinitum that nothing ever gets resolved. At Nueva the kids were, on average, too eager to get to the Answer as fast as possible and move on to the next thing. I think I dealt with this successfully at Nueva; I think I did a very good job pushing the kids to be slower and more thoughtful and think about process and reasoning. It usually only took a few weeks at the start of each semester to build that classroom intellectual culture. At Deep Springs the vice is the opposite: the endless graduate seminar that never makes any progress. We had, for instance, a 40-minute discussion of what boils down to de Morgan’s laws for propositional logic (“not (both p and q)” being the same as “(not p) or (not q)”). In my mind this is straightforward, but the kids just wouldn’t stop talking about it, even after (so it appeared to me) they had resolved the question. Sometimes questions do have answers, guys! Quod erat demonstrandum demonstravimus!

The kids have weaker technical skills, mathematically, than the Nueva kids, even the kids here who think of themselves as more math/sciencey. That’s a blessing, because it makes things easier for me. I otherwise feel intimidated by all these Committee on Social Thought Ph.D. colleagues teaching 300 pages of Spinoza a week! On the other hand, I haven’t gotten a single complaint about assigning too much homework (at Nueva this was perpetual background noise, not too loud and easy to ignore but always present). Nor have I gotten any complaints about the problems I write being too ambiguous, or kids not already knowing how to get everything perfectly 100% right. I mean none of that as a slur on my beloved Nueva kids (and it was only ever a tiny percentage who said that stuff); it’s just… the anxiety here is so much lower. Everyone is very busy, and everyone is very passionate, but no one is having panic attacks because they got something wrong. Shit. I wasn’t expecting this to be a difference. I had gotten used to math-anxiety-therapist being a core part of my job. I want kids so badly; I like living in the Bay Area; I really DON’T want to raise kids in the Bay Area. Hard to compete with the Nueva extremes at either end. Teaching multivariable calculus to seventh graders is pretty cool; jumping in front of Caltrain is not.

The other salient similarity to Nueva is that the kids all have very strong opinions on how they want to be taught. The Dean told me a story from last semester: his class read some article by some well-known guy, and the guy came in to talk to the class (maybe it was over Zoom? idk), and before the guy even had a chance to say anything, the students launched into a discussion of the article. Ninety minutes later, time was up, the guy had never had a chance to say anything, and the students all thanked him profusely for such a great class. Don, a beloved elderly visiting professor who’s teaching the Wittgenstein class, told me how yesterday in class he made what he thought was a brief comment, and one of the kids scolded him: “Don! You’re talking too much!” The kids want self-directed seminars; they’re suspicious of authority.

So Deep Springs feels like a weird amalgam of all my past educational experiences. There’s the close reading of classic texts and intense seminar discussions of Veritas and UChicago, coupled with the student-directedness of Nueva. Not a synthesis I was expecting to ever see. Allan Bloom’s ghost haunts every generation; kids always want to throw out Thucydides in favor of the fashionable. Somehow, at Deep Springs, at least in the current incarnation, the synthesis works.

The other faculty are amazing and wonderful; their C.V.s intimidate me but in person they’re so kind, brilliant, and down-to-earth; everyone has connections to at least two of UChicago, St. John’s, and Cornell. The Cornell connection is funny: I don’t think of Cornell as being a particularly intellectual school, but there are a lot of historical ties between it and Deep Springs. This morning I went “into town” to run a 5K in Bishop (23:15; 10th out of 73, ehh).
