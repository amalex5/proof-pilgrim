Bishop, California

I taught two classes this semester, but they ended together, in a conjoined culminating dinner party seminar where we talked about Gödel and incompleteness and the infinite and ate Xi’an Famous Foods homemade hand-pulled noodles over cumin beef.

The beef was from the cow we slaughtered last month. The seminar was on Logicomix, the frame-story-within-a-frame-story graphic novel about Bertrand Russell and the quest for the foundations of logic and mathematics. Between pulling and slapping noodles, the kids talked about the book for a solid hour and a half. The only thing I did was serve cups of milky chai. (The milk was from Deep Springs cows, of course). DS kids and texts are like Nueva kids and math problems. Give them reading and they’ll do it, intensely and thoroughly, no matter how dense, no matter how long. Come class time they’ll talk about it until they fall asleep.

(The kids didn’t fall asleep in my living room after the dinner party. They had to get back to pulling all-nighters for their final papers.)

The formal end-of-term dinner was a few days later. The kids staged an elaborate Elizabethan feast. Two feasts, actually, sorted sartorially into separate buffet lines. The dress code: come as a peasant or as a lord. On the peasants’ table: bowls of lettuce; sheet pans of home fries and carrots. On the lords’ table: mincemeat pies, multiple loaves of aspic, warm rolls and fresh cheese from the DS cows, strawberry-rhubarb crisp, two dozen turkey legs (each the size of my forearm), an entire lamb, roasted and supine.

Colleague Aaron looked at one of the kids dressed in a grubby tunic. “I’m not sure if Kel is wearing a costume,” he said, “or if she just came from working on the farm.”

After classes ended it became “Shakespeare Week.” The kids staged Hamlet on Friday, all of it, on-book, in field #4, still muddy from last week’s rains. They used a cow skull for Yorick’s. Thursday night Aaron hosted a screening of Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead. Wednesday night student Ruby hosted a screening of an episode of Slings and Arrows—some 2000s Canadian TV show I’d never heard of, about an unsuccessful Shakespeare troupe in a fictionalized Stratford Festival. Each season follows the troupe as they put on one of the Bard’s plays; the themes and plots reflect the play the troupe is putting on.

Perfect semester. No notes.

There was a bat infestation in the main building. During Amia Srinavansan’s final lecture one was hanging upside down from the air vent above her head. Everyone else could see it. Some guys came to band the great-horned owl chicks in the nest by the dairy barn. They were in a Forest Service truck but their actual affiliation was ambiguous. The chick clicked and clicked (that’s their vocalization) and the guys insisted that it was OK for us to hold it. There’s been a coyote hanging out in the north fields. A wolf hanging out in Bishop. The first wolf in Inyo County in a century! It has a GPS collar and its position (blurred) gets updated on CDFW’s webpage every day. Driving home from Bishop I saw a herd of elk right by the side of 395, lit up in the sunset with the Caltech radio telescopes and the White Mountains behind them.

Sasha got stopped at TSA because she had a live .22 round in her backpack. Harper had given it to her for Christmas. Sasha had forgotten. Harper is one of several students with a gun on campus; she’s a tiny second-generation Marxist from Portland. Sasha was standing at the checkpoint sobbing, the officer threatening to arrest her if she couldn’t find the bullet, ripping apart her backpack, boarding ending in five minutes, only one flight a day out of Bishop, her only way of getting to her grandmother’s funeral.

Bishop and gear stores is like 90s college towns and bookstores. There are a half-dozen of them, new and used, each with a different personality.

There’s no paper recycling in Inyo County. Plastic, glass, aluminum; not paper. Luca joked about bringing his broken-down Amazon boxes back to Marin to recycle. Like a normal college student bringing their laundry home.

The milk surfeit continues. The dairy boys bought Nesquik powder to make quarts of strawberry and chocolate milk so people will drink more. Mango lassis have started being served with every dinner.

Brendan had a dream that his transcript got sent to the colleges he’s applying to transfer to and it showed him getting an A- in Adam Smith and a C in logic. He went to the dean, who roared, “Andrew Alexander is the best math teacher in the universe! If he gave you a C, you deserve a C!” (In reality Brendan did fine.)

One of the kids organized an end-of-semester pickleball tournament. I entered. I’ve never played pickleball. I was the first person to be eliminated. I’m #1!!!!

Grizzled old ranch manager Tim killed a rattlesnake with a shovel. It was sunning itself on the steps of the main building. I got there in time to see it still writhing, decapitated, for several minutes.

Two creative projects this semester. The first I called “Psychedelic Zippers.” It came out of our discussion of integration by parts. “Oh,” I realized walking through the desert late at night under the full moon. “It’s just a giant zipper you can zip up in two ways.” The resulting “installation piece”––dangling chains of paper taped together––hung in the Main Room for a week. The second was to make homemade sagebrush smudge sticks. I cut springtime shoots, supple and velvety and teal, bundled them together, and let them hang dry for a few weeks. Thanks to our commercial vacuum-sealer I now have 16 vacuum-packed sagebrush smudges, with handwritten labels, ready as this year’s Christmas gifts! I love incense; I love sagebrush; this is a project I’ve wanted to do for years. Really leaning into my inner Berkeley/Ithaca hippie.

According to Strava I skiied a quarter-million vertical feet this winter.

Two highlights from Old Movie Night recently. A 1970s West German film directed by Rainier Fassbinder in which an elderly housecleaner falls in love with young Moroccan gästarbeiter (Fear Eats the Soul). The love is strange but real; the racism and xenophobia the protagonists experience (directly, and by nuptial proxy) felt discouragingly modern. Second is a 1960s French film, Au hazard Balthazar, which (quoting my colleague’s description here):

follows the donkey Balthazar as he is passed from owner to owner, some kind and some cruel but all with motivations beyond his understanding. Balthazar, whose life parallels that of his first keeper, Marie, is truly a beast of burden, suffering the sins of humankind. But despite his powerlessness, he accepts his fate nobly. Through Bresson’s unconventional approach to composition, sound, and narrative, this simple story becomes a moving parable about purity and transcendence … If you’re taking “Christian Thought” you won’t want to miss this!

(The faculty member who sponsored this is Modern Orthodox and taught Maimonides last semester.)

“Should I truth-table my whole life?,” Sasha asked, a few weeks into the semester. At the closing dinner seminar: “You’d think it would be easier to prove the foundations of calculus than to prove that God exists.”

Victoria: “I feel like calculus is the perfect balance between following rules and formulas and breaking them by finding loopholes, violently.”

I went to the BH to make the six pounds of dough for the hand-pulled noodles. As I was working it at the baking station Will came in to fry up one of last night’s cannoli from the walk-in. Haley came in and they started talking about Spinoza. Harper came in and the conversation transitioned to Wittgenstein. Harper wandered out, and the conversation wandered towards Hegel. Meanwhile the kitchen started filling up with smoke. I walked over to Will and Haley. A few feet behind them, on the ten-burner commercial range, the cast-iron pot for Will’s cannoli was boiling off huge clouds of smoke. I’ve never seen oil smoke that much. I was too amused to intervene, so I went back to my dough, and neither Will or Haley noticed until the fire alarm went off.

Jacob, Don, and I did a rare turn doing labor. We volunteered to do the dishes after the end-of-term dinner. That way the kids could have their final student body meeting all together and uninterrupted. After such a blowout meal it took three hours for the three of us to clean every last pot and pan and scrub down every surface. Did the kids really use every single sheet pan to make dinner? How many sheet pans do we own? 25?

I only learned I had volunteered when I saw Don’s email to the community announcing as much. Don is a retired philosophy of science professor in his late 60s, with never-ending amounts of energy and optimism. He didn’t get his PhD until he was almost 40 but still managed to have a 20-year career at (of all places) the National University of Singapore. He’s done a bunch of semesters as a visiting professor here the last few years and loves it. Jacob is my age, went to Deep Springs, then to Yale, then got an MFA. His book of poems won an award but he scrapes by on odd high-school teaching jobs (and his wife’s postdoc). As a student, being on the crew that washes the dishes in the boardinghouse after every meal was his favorite labor position. It always turned into a high-energy dance party fueled by detergent, Hillary Duff, and Lindsay Lohan. He was eager to re-create that scene and put on his 20-year-old playlist. It’s the same shitty pop that fueled my own mid-2000s liaisons:

You always dress in yellow when you wanna dress in gold
Instead of listening to your heart, you do just what you’re told
You keep waiting where you are, for what, you’ll never know
Let’s just get into your car and go, baby, go

To quit Nueva and come here for a semester felt, beforehand, like both a big risk and the right choice. In hindsight, my time has been so amazing that it absolutely has to have been the right choice. But how that will manifest in the medium-term I don’t know. The last two things for next year from which I hadn’t yet been rejected—one of which was a stretch but a total dream; the other of which seemed fun and I thought was a sure thing—I was rejected from on the same day in the space of two hours. (“Give me a hug,” said college president Andy, about whom I just can’t say enough good things.) Last time I quit a job I loved because I knew I needed something different—the heartbreak of ending something that’s good but isn’t enough—I was thinking of Eliot’s lines from the end of East Coker:

We must be still and still moving
Into another intensity
For a further union, a deeper communion
Through the dark cold and the empty desolation

This time I’m thinking about a couplet earlier in East Coker: “For us there is only the trying / the rest is not our business.” The professional and personal paralysis I inflicted on myself in my 20s was the result of not even trying. But it’s only ever about the process. It’s never about the results. Isn’t that what I’m always telling my students? The next poem in the Quartets, The Dry Salvages, ends on the same theme:

And right action is freedom
From past and future also.
For most of us, this is the aim
Never here to be realised;
Who are only undefeated
Because we have gone on trying;
We, content at the last
If our temporal reversion nourish
(Not too far from the yew-tree)
The life of significant soil.

love,
Andrew
